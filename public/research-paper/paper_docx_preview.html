Explanation Reliability and Failure Detection in Phishing URL Classification Under Dataset Shift

Kasim Ishaque Ghanchi | Department of Computer Engineering | Mumbai, India

kasimghanchi672@gmail.com | ORCID: 0009-0007-7444-9977

Abstract

High accuracy on a random test split does not establish that a phishing URL classifier will remain reliable on new domains or data collected through different pipelines. Nor does a plausible feature-attribution plot establish that its explanation is faithful to the classifier. This paper develops an auditable comparative protocol for evaluating prediction quality, explanation reliability, and the usefulness of explanations for identifying classification failures under dataset shift. It specifies matched lexical inputs; domain-disjoint and cross-corpus evaluation; logistic-regression and tree-ensemble baselines; SHAP and LIME explanation audits; and a held-out comparison of confidence-only, explanation-only, and combined error-warning rules. A descriptive comparison of two published datasets exposes a substantial class-prior difference: the published counts imply 62.9% phishing URLs in LegitPhish and 10.4% in URL-Phish. This difference motivates prior-aware evaluation and explicit checks for source artifacts. The resulting protocol identifies testable hypotheses, leakage controls, effect-size reporting, and a practical evidence standard for responsible deployment. This version reports verified literature-derived dataset statistics and a structured experimental plan; no new model-training or explanation-performance results are claimed.

Introduction

Phishing URL classifiers often operate in an environment where suspicious domains, URL construction practices, and data acquisition processes change faster than model retraining cycles. An apparently strong benchmark result may therefore depend on accidental properties of how its dataset was assembled. Rashid et al. documented impaired cross-dataset generalization in URL detection and showed that collection-related URL patterns can influence predictions [5]. A later study identified duplication, inconsistent labels, and domain overrepresentation as additional threats to benchmarking [6]. These problems affect not just the classifier, but also the interpretation of its decisions.

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) can expose influential URL features. LIME approximates a model locally through a simple surrogate [1]; SHAP assigns additive feature contributions using a principled framework [2]. However, an explanation can be repeatable without being faithful, or intuitive without diagnosing when a model will fail. Randomization-based scrutiny has revealed limitations of visually convincing attribution methods in other domains [3]. Fidelity and sensitivity consequently warrant measurement instead of impressionistic inspection [4].

Recent phishing research already compares attribution techniques or examines temporal feature extraction [11, 12, 13]. Merely drawing SHAP and LIME charts is thus not a sufficient contribution. The narrower question developed here is whether independently evaluated explanation-quality signals add useful information to confidence-based failure warnings on unseen domains and datasets.

The contributions of this research-protocol manuscript are: (i) a leakage-aware comparison across group-disjoint and source-shift settings; (ii) an explicit audit of explanation faithfulness and reproducibility; (iii) a label-isolated evaluation of explanation-aware failure screening against strong uncertainty baselines; and (iv) a descriptive, independently verifiable audit of class balance in two recently published URL corpora. These are protocol and corpus-summary contributions, not experimental performance claims.

Related Work and Research Gap

URL classification under shift

A URL’s length, punctuation, hostname structure, and character composition offer inexpensive signals for phishing detection. Dataset composition can nevertheless create shortcuts: for example, a crawler may collect benign HTTPS URLs more often than malicious HTTPS URLs. Rashid et al. analyzed dataset-specific URL-prefix effects in cross-dataset experiments [5]. Domain reuse and duplicate URLs further inflate randomly split scores, particularly when the same registered domain appears in train and test partitions [6].

Feature attribution is not operational trust

LIME and SHAP are complementary model-explanation tools [1, 2]. Panthi et al. compared these approaches for boosted phishing classifiers [11]; Mayo et al. investigated a temporally dynamic phishing feature pipeline with both methods [12]. Another IEEE study assessed LIME across multiple classifiers using stability- and fidelity-related criteria [13]. These works establish both the utility of local attribution and the risk of duplicating an explanation-only comparison.

From probability to selective warnings

Calibration evaluates whether probability estimates reflect observed frequencies; prediction confidence can be misleading even when top-label accuracy is high [9]. Selective classification allows a system to defer cases in exchange for lower error among the cases it does handle [10]. The open empirical issue for this study is incremental value: does adding explanation-quality information improve error detection or selective risk at matched coverage, after confidence and dataset shift are controlled?

Research Questions and Hypotheses

RQ1: How much do predictive quality and calibration change between ordinary random, registered-domain-disjoint, and cross-corpus tests? H1: Cross-corpus evaluation will generally be more challenging than a random in-source split, although the direction and magnitude must be measured.

RQ2: Do SHAP and LIME explanations remain faithful and reproducible when the evaluation population changes? H2: Their rankings and perturbation responses may differ across model families and test sources; the protocol does not presuppose a winner.

RQ3: Can explanation-derived evidence identify misclassified URLs better than confidence alone? H3: A combined rule may add information, but its incremental benefit must be demonstrated with paired uncertainty intervals and held-out tests.

RQ4: Are models exploiting dataset-source artifacts rather than phishing-relevant structure? H4: Predictable source identity from lexical features, or sharp feature-prevalence differences, would signal a confounding risk rather than prove causal shortcut use.

A numerical inconsistency requires disclosure: the two LegitPhish class counts sum to 101,218, whereas its published text also reports a total of 101,219 [7]. The plot and table therefore use the component counts and neither silently repair nor explain away the one-record discrepancy. Raw-file verification is mandatory before modeling. Cross-corpus comparisons should share a freshly recomputed feature extractor rather than rely on differently implemented supplied columns.

Proposed Experimental Design

Data integrity and splits

After obtaining the files from their cited repositories, retain raw URL strings and original labels, log version and checksum, strip exact duplicate URLs, and flag contradictory labels. Canonicalization should be conservative because rewriting URL paths or queries can alter real behavior. Normalize scheme and host case for grouping only, preserving the original URL for feature extraction. Use a maintained public-suffix list to derive registered-domain groups; discard malformed examples under a recorded rule. Partition training, tuning, and in-source testing by registered domain, then test on the independent corpus after removing train/test domain collisions. When collection timestamps are trustworthy, add a forward-time experiment; do not imply a temporal test merely because papers were published in different years.


Fig. 2. Planned research workflow: domain-separated training, explanation audits, and cross-corpus evaluation. No model scores are shown.

Features, models, and controls

Compute the same lexical and structural features from both corpora: URL and hostname lengths; counts of dots, digits, hyphens, separators, and special symbols; query length; URL character entropy; subdomain count; IP-literal indicator; and explicit HTTPS scheme indicator. Avoid WHOIS age, live page content, reputation lookups, and blacklist membership in the primary benchmark because they require source-dependent acquisition at prediction time and can silently leak labels. Include three transparent-to-complex baselines: standardized logistic regression, random forest, and gradient-boosted trees. Tune on group-disjoint validation data with a fixed budget and publish the search space, seeds, and hardware.

Explanations and faithfulness checks

Use model-appropriate SHAP implementations and model-agnostic LIME under declared backgrounds, random seeds, perturbation budgets, and sample counts. Compare top-k feature overlap and rank correlation on the same feature representation. A deletion test replaces top-ranked features with training-distribution reference values and measures prediction change against random-feature deletion. Replacing correlated URL features independently can create implausible feature vectors; deletion is therefore a model-probe proxy rather than proof of causal importance. Report faithfulness tests and explanation runtime, including cases where an explainer fails.

For URL observation x, classification model f, attribution vector a(x), and top-k masked feature set Mₖ(x), define the magnitude of the deletion response as follows:

Dₖ(x) = | f(x) − f(Tₘₖ(x)) | (1)

Here, T is a declared reference-value transformation. Compare ΔDₖ = Dₖ − Dₖ(random) with paired uncertainty intervals; a large unadjusted change does not establish explanation validity. For repeatability, calculate top-k Jaccard agreement across independent LIME runs. For deterministic SHAP, repeat the audit using alternative admissible background samples.

Error-warning ablation

Define eᵢ = 1 when the predicted class differs from the true class and eᵢ = 0 otherwise. On validation data only, fit a low-capacity model predicting eᵢ from A) confidence or entropy; B) attribution stability and deletion response; and C) their combination. Compare all three on untouched test examples. Explanation features may depend on input xᵢ, trained model outputs, and reference data, but must never use test labels during feature construction. Fit calibration on held-out tuning data and freeze it before cross-corpus evaluation. The primary comparison is:

ΔAUROC = AUROC(s꜀, e) − AUROC(sₐ, e) (2)

Here, s꜀ is the combined error-risk score, and sₐ is the confidence-only risk score. A positive point estimate requires a paired confidence interval and tests in both transfer directions before it supports a claim of improvement.

Reporting metrics and statistical analysis

For classification, report phishing precision, recall, F1, average precision, AUROC, false-positive rate, and confusion matrices under both native and explicitly adjusted class prevalences. For probability quality, report Brier scores and reliability diagrams; calibration learned in one domain may not hold after dataset shift [9]. For deferral, rank examples by warning score and report error risk R(c) within retained coverage c, matched-coverage differences, and area under the risk-coverage curve [10]. For explanation quality, report deletion-minus-random response, repeated-seed stability, top-k agreement, and runtime per example.

Replicate domain splits across at least five predefined seeds, retaining domain grouping in resampling. Calculate bootstrap 95% intervals by resampling registered-domain clusters, not individual near-duplicate URLs. Show effect sizes and paired differences; avoid using uncorrected multiple significance tests as the principal evidence. Ablate URL components that could betray source collection (e.g., HTTPS prevalence) and fit a source-identity classifier on the common lexical features as a diagnostic. A strong source discriminator alone is not proof that the phishing model used a specific spurious shortcut.

Study Status and Reporting Boundaries

The present document is a reproducible comparative study design supported by a literature-derived corpus audit. It deliberately contains no newly measured F1 scores, AUROC gains, stability coefficients, SHAP importance rankings, or risk-coverage outcomes. Producing any such value without downloading the datasets, executing the split and model pipelines, and retaining logs would be fabrication. The attached executable starter pipeline creates actual performance plots only when supplied with licensed source files and explicitly specified label encodings.

Table 2 states the evidence required for a defensible subsequent results version. A valid negative result—for instance, no benefit from explanations beyond calibrated confidence—would be scientifically informative and should be reported rather than suppressed. The main novelty, if empirically supported, would be evidence about incremental explanation utility under shift, not invention of SHAP, LIME, or a generic phishing classifier.

Threats to Validity and Responsible Use

Selection and label bias. Both candidate datasets depend on particular benign and malicious collection practices; transfer scores mix differences in class prevalence, collection time, and source pipelines. Neither corpus alone establishes deployment prevalence. Contradictory labels and duplicated domains must be quantified.

Explanation perturbation artifacts. Feature deletion can leave the manifold of valid URLs, and SHAP explanations depend on the choice of background and feature dependence assumptions. Stability is not causality, and explanation agreement need not mean correctness. Human comprehensibility would require a separate reader study.

Security and privacy. Examples are analyzed as strings; the benchmark must not visit suspicious URLs or resolve malicious hosts. The shared reproducibility package should avoid posting active harmful links in rendered figures, remove personal data accidentally embedded in URLs, and preserve source attribution. Results can support analyst triage but should not be presented as an autonomous safety guarantee.

Evaluation honesty. A paper becomes an empirical conference submission only when experiments and validation are complete. Working code, environment versions, dataset checksums, excluded records, exact split groups, random seeds, and plots should accompany any numerical claims. Sponsorship or acceptance decisions are outside the scope of research results.

Conclusion

Responsible phishing detection requires more than accurate predictions and attractive attribution graphics. This paper specifies a testable comparison that separates model performance, explanation faithfulness, and confidence-conditioned failure screening, and it makes domain and dataset shift first-class evaluation settings. Published counts from two candidate datasets already reveal materially different phishing prevalences, underscoring the need for careful priors and source-leakage checks. Whether explanations genuinely improve detection of model errors remains an empirical question. Answering it with leakage-safe data splits, measured explanation fidelity, paired confidence baselines, and reproducible evidence is the next step toward a defensible CYCRAI submission.
